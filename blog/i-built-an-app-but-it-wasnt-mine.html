I built an app, but it wasn’t mine

In 2025, “learn Swift” was on my goals list with no actionable plan attached. Obviously, nothing happened. So for 2026, I got serious. Quarterly milestones, a weekly schedule, a concrete target for Q1: learn the basics and build a simple expense logger that works on my phone. The bigger dream is a full personal finance tracker tailored to my use cases, but Q1 was just about getting started.

In late January, I made TapMoji, a tap-to-change emoji app with sound effects, bounce animations, and a theme system. I didn’t write a single line of code, I used Claude Code to build the whole thing because it felt like the more obvious move than starting from scratch manually. And honestly, it was exhilarating! After a full year of wanting to make an iOS app and making zero progress, I went from nothing to an actual working app on my phone in an afternoon. I tested it on my phone, iterated on it, and showed it to people. It had state management, audio playback, persistent preferences — real programming concepts. I was having fun and was pretty proud too seeing this tiny idea come to life.

After a week or so tweaking TapMoji, I decided to take on something more substantial. I discovered that Dime — my favorite budget tracking app — is open source, so I thought: what if I fork it, apply the new Liquid Glass UI from iOS 26, fix this one bug that’s been crashing the app every time I tap something in the chart view, and make it my own little version?

I spent two hours with Claude Code. Got the local build running, stripped out stuff I didn’t need, gave the app Liquid Glass UI. Errors kept coming up, one after another, and Claude kept fixing them. Some of them persisted, some went away and came back again, then again. Luckily I was more stubborn, so eventually I had a working version on my phone, and even got the bug fixed! It seriously felt amazing to hold something I had only imagined.

And then something strange happened. I felt satisfied for about five seconds, and then immediately the feeling vanished. With TapMoji, I didn’t feel this way — it was my idea, a simple app, and vibe coding it felt fine. But this was someone else’s complex, polished project. I had no idea what most of the files in the Xcode project did. If I wanted to change something as simple as a border radius or a font size, I wouldn’t have known which file to open. I could read the code and understand what each section was for, but I couldn’t have written any of it from scratch. I did it, but it’s not mine. It’s someone else’s hard work, and I just took it and pointed AI at it. I wasn’t going to publish it, I was just going to use it as my own personal version. But even that didn’t feel right. At all.

If I’m really honest with myself: I wanted fast results. Learning iOS development from scratch can take months, probably years, and I knew that going in. The learning curve would be nothing like HTML and CSS, which is what I’m comfortable with and can write from scratch. When AI made it possible to get working apps on my phone in a day, I took the shortcut. I could have been more patient, stuck to the schedule I made for myself, and accepted that the first few months would feel slow and unglamorous. Instead I reached for the fast version and ended up with apps I can’t explain and a quarter of very little substantial iOS development knowledge. I have no one to blame for that.

And the thing is, I can’t just ignore AI either. It is incredibly useful. I use it to improve this very website. But there’s a difference between using AI when you already understand what you’re building and using it to skip the understanding entirely. I did the latter, and I got the apps I wanted but nothing much in my head.

I wanted to call myself an iOS developer, but it turns out that having apps on your phone doesn’t make you one when you went into it 99% blind about what was going to be underneath. Vibe coding doesn’t work for me. How long it takes to get to where I actually want to be — to be able to call myself an iOS developer, whatever that even means — well, I don’t have any idea yet. But I’m sitting with the uncomfortable slow part now, and I’ll find my way there.

Eventually. Maybe. We’ll see.
