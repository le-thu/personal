What craft means when making gets easier

Around May this year, I started using dictation for my journal entries. I’d open Superwhisper and just talk without worrying about how I sounded or whether I was making sense. I just started a big project at work with a lot of ambiguity and back and forth, and the entries became daily notes to myself: how I felt, what I was thinking, what to try next. It was also useful when I was traveling and wanted to capture an experience while the details were still fresh.

By September, I’d mostly stopped. Talking was a good way to get thoughts out of my head, but it left a growing pile of material I hadn’t really looked at closely. And it wasn’t just the entries since May. I had years of material going back to 2020.

Going back through the pile #

I’ve been journaling since 2019. The early entries were about work. By 2020, I had warmed up to writing about my personal life, and those entries were — and still are — just for me. I didn’t start publishing on this blog until October 2024. Many posts started with a memory or a particular experience I wanted to make sense of or simply relish. Over time, I became more interested in connecting the dots across entries, why I reacted a certain way, and what patterns kept repeating.

AI changed how I could do that. I’d export a few months of entries and ask it to look for patterns, connections, or things I might have missed. Sometimes it surfaced a connection I had overlooked. Other times it told me something I already knew, or read too much into an observation. Whichever it was, I had to decide what was worth pursuing, fill in what it had missed, and work out what I actually wanted to say. The ideas were rarely new to me, and often they were things I had already thought about but hadn’t quite put together. But the distance between having an idea and turning it into something I could publish got a lot shorter.

When exploration gets cheaper #

I’ve noticed a similar change in my work as a product designer.

There’s usually a stretch of thinking before I get into the design itself. When customer feedback comes in, my first instinct is to understand what is actually going on, whether the problem affects other people, and what else might solve it, rather than take the request at face value. AI has become a big part of that stretch. With Claude Code and Figma MCP, I can talk through an idea, try different, bolder directions, turn them into prototypes, and move between code and Figma without doing every step manually.

I can now try things I probably wouldn’t have bothered with before simply because they would have taken too much time. It’s been really fun. It also makes me wonder how many of those things are worth doing, and how many I do just because I finally can. The cost used to do some of the deciding for me, and now that’s on me. The deciding also used to be spread out since I made choices as I went, while laying out the UI and doing things by hand, whereas now the ideas arrive all at once and the decisions come with them. I like this new way of working, even though some days it’s still a lot.

What craft means now #

So the pattern is: making got cheaper and deciding didn’t. Which leaves me wondering how much of the process I need to do myself for the finished work to feel like mine.

Before AI, I’d write a draft and then revise it over and over, moving sentences around, finding better words, and spending far too long trying to get something just right. Now it depends on what I’m starting with. If I already know what the post is about, I give AI a few months of journal entries and ask it to pull together a first draft from my own words, run-on sentences and all. If I don’t, I ask for an outline instead. Reading the bullet points brings back things I’d forgotten, so I keep adding, and the draft evolves from that back and forth. Either way, the draft changes a lot before I consider it done.

That isn’t the same as handing a finished draft to a human editor, I know that. The words are mine but the first arrangement isn’t, and arranging used to be where a lot of the thinking happened. I still do that thinking, it just all lands in the editing now instead of being spread across the whole process.

Anyway, I don’t think the answer to that question is an amount anymore. What I care about is whether the finished piece reflects what I think and what I mean.

I may not be crafting every letter or pixel myself, but I’m the one deciding what belongs and what doesn’t. I reject suggestions, correct misunderstandings, move things around, and rewrite passages that don’t sound right. I still notice the slightly misaligned element, the awkward spacing, or the UX copy that sounds odd, convoluted, or unclear. AI has made some of those easier to fix, though what I notice is the same as before. And even when the first draft comes more easily, deciding what deserves to stay is still work.

I want to write more, understand myself a little better, and put things into the world that might make someone else curious, and I don’t want to spend hours producing every sentence from scratch to get there.

No one way #

For longer pieces, I now use iA Writer (it used to be Obsidian). Apple Journal works well for capturing everyday thoughts, but iA Writer feels like a calm place for me to spend time when I have more to say, like this post.

It reminds me of why I keep going back to photography with a dedicated camera. My phone can do the job, but sometimes I want the experience of using a camera.

Typing a journal entry can do the same thing. It’s slower, and sometimes that’s exactly why I do it.

I don’t think one approach has to replace the other. There are days when I want to capture a thought before it disappears, or take a rough draft and see what I can make of it, and days when I’d rather sit with a blank page and find the words myself.

I’m using AI to work on this post, too. Even as I sit here editing in iA Writer, I’m asking AI where the argument is weak, which parts are filler, and which sentences aren’t working. I like having that help. I also know I wouldn’t be spending this much time editing by hand, sentence by sentence, if I didn’t care how the piece turned out. And I enjoy it.

I think AI is a good tool and it makes me want to work. It doesn’t automatically make the work good, though, so that part is still mine.
