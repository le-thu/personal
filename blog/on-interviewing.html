On interviewing

At this point in my career, I’ve been the interviewer far more often than I’ve been the interviewee, and that kind of puts things in perspective. It wasn’t a milestone I or anyone marked for me, I just noticed one day that I’m the one opening the call, setting the tone, and carrying a decision that affects someone else’s career.

Most of what I’ve read about interviews is written for the candidate. How to answer, how to tell your story, how to manage the nerves. There’s probably a whole world of resources for interviewers out there that I just haven’t come across, but the assumption I always had was that the candidate has the hard job, and the interviewer just shows up with questions. That hasn’t exactly been my experience in the last few years. Being an interviewer is also being a moderator. You’re facilitating the conversation, listening for how someone actually thinks, asking questions that hopefully give you the signal you need, managing the clock, managing yourself, and managing the other person’s nerves, all at the same time. It’s just as hard as being interviewed, if not harder.

The two sides carry different weights too. The candidate carries the emotional pressure: decoding the intent behind each question, picking the right example on the spot, and presenting an accurate picture of themselves while their heart rate says otherwise (I can attest). The interviewer carries the cognitive load. In a general round, I’m following someone’s story, watching how they think, whether they lead the conversation, whether curiosity shows up in the questions they ask back. In a whiteboard round, I’m probing their choices, reminding them to think out loud, nudging them to make assumptions and ask questions instead of designing in silence.

I’ve never really been able to take notes in real time, because I want to be fully present with the candidate. So I keep a mental log of bullet points, then sit down right after the interview to write my evaluation while everything is still fresh. Sometimes I make the call right there, other times I sleep on it and compare reads the next day with whoever ran the other round. Only then do we decide whether to move forward.

All of that and it still rests on a single hour of conversation. There’s no way you can really know a person from just talking to them for an hour. Kind of a strange thing to hold, because the people I ended up most excited to hire were often the ones I had a good feeling about within the first conversation. I don’t fully trust that feeling and I don’t fully dismiss it either. The best I can do is treat it as a hypothesis and spend the hour looking for evidence.

If the signal is thin, I think more of the responsibility sits with the interviewer because we’re the ones who set the conditions. A candidate who gets interrupted, rushed, and asked rapid-fire checklist or shallow questions will look worse than they are, and we’ll walk away with a false read. So when an interview goes badly, the question is more “Did I give them a real chance to show me how they think?” than “Was this a weak candidate?”

Not every bad interview has a villain though. I’ve seen candidates who excelled in the general or whiteboard round struggle when they present to a panel, not because they can’t think, but because they’re trying to do too much. Designers sometimes want to show off their product or technical chops to prove how capable they are to the point it ends up diluting the one thing we’re actually evaluating: can this person design, and will they fight for the user? I’ve watched people fall into that trap, and it’s hard to pull them out of it mid-interview especially if they’ve already committed to that direction or scripted the whole thing that way.

Styles and preferences collide as well. Some of my colleagues prefer short answers that get straight to the point. I like to think I’m adaptable to different personalities, but when a candidate sets up a lot of context before arriving at their answer, I can feel the room’s patience thinning. Neither side is wrong, the conversation just never finds a rhythm. It’s just a mismatch, not failure. Working styles, personality, chemistry, honestly they play a bigger part than any scorecard admits.

We once made a candidate wait on an offer for another week, for reasons that felt sound on our side of the table. I did keep thinking to myself: what if the situation was reversed? How would we feel, waiting? We’re evaluating them, but they’re also deciding whether to trust us. They waited and accepted, and everything was good, until it wasn’t. Within a year, we parted ways despite real effort from both sides. That’s a story for another day, but what it essentially was is this: everyone was confident, the interviews went well, and we were still wrong. Since then the pendulum has swung the other way, we’ve gotten stricter, maybe a bit too vigilant, which I’ve realized is its own kind of bias to watch for.

I think about this today because someone once sat where I sit now and took a chance on me, before I even fully believed in myself. That interview changed my life, more than a little. Most interviews won’t, I never know which one will, and neither does the person on the other side of the table. We have the responsibility to make the time we have with each other count anyway.
